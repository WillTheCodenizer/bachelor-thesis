\chapter{Results} \label{ch:results}

The angular power spectra derived in \autoref{ch:methodology} are theoretical predictions for one
particular choice of model parameters. In order to quantify how well these parameters could be
recovered from an actual measurement, the likelihood of the observed spectra is assumed to be
Gaussian in the parameters in the vicinity of the fiducial model. Under this assumption the expected
parameter covariance follows directly from the Fisher information matrix, which is the standard tool
for cosmological forecasts. The true posterior may of course be broader and non-Gaussian, so that
the uncertainties quoted in this chapter should be understood as an optimistic estimate of what a
given survey combination can achieve.

\section{Fisher Matrix} \label{sec:fisher_matrix}
The Fisher matrix measures how sensitively the data react to a variation of the model parameters and
therefore how much information a given measurement carries about them~\cite{Tegmark_1997}. Since
conducting an experiment is costly, such a forecast is a useful tool to assess the merit of a
proposed survey configuration before any data are taken. For a set of tracer fields whose harmonic
coefficients are Gaussian distributed, the Fisher matrix of the angular power spectra is given
by~\cite{Tegmark_1997, Reischke_2021}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fisher}
    F_{ij}
    =
    f_{\mathrm{sky}}
    \sum_{\ell=\ell_{\mathrm{min}}}^{\ell_{\mathrm{max}}}
    \frac{2\ell+1}{2}\,
    \operatorname{Tr}
    \left[
        \hat{C}_{\ell}^{-1}\,
        \frac{\partial C_{\ell}}{\partial \theta_i}\,
        \hat{C}_{\ell}^{-1}\,
        \frac{\partial C_{\ell}}{\partial \theta_j}
    \right],
\end{equation}

where $\theta_i$ denotes the $i$-th model parameter and the trace runs over the tracer indices. The
factor $(2\ell+1)/2$ counts the number of independent modes available at each multipole, and the sky
fraction $f_{\mathrm{sky}}$ accounts for the fact that only a part of the celestial sphere is
observed.

The matrix $\hat{C}_{\ell}$ is the covariance of the measured fields, that is the sum of the
theoretical signal and the noise contribution of \autoref{eq:shot_noise},
$\hat{C}_{\ell} = C_{\ell} + N_{\ell}$. Two configurations are evaluated. In the FRB-only forecast
the data vector consists of the FRB auto-spectrum alone, so that $\hat{C}_{\ell}$ reduces to the
scalar $C_{\ell}^{\mathrm{FRB}} + \bar{n}_{\mathrm{FRB}}^{-1}$ and the trace in \autoref{eq:fisher}
becomes an ordinary product. In the joint forecast the data vector contains the six tomographic
galaxy bins together with the FRB sample, ordered as $(g_1, \dots, g_6, \mathrm{FRB})$, which yields
the $7\times7$ matrix

\begin{equation}\label{eq:covariance_blocks}
    \hat{C}_{\ell}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        C_{\ell}^{\,g_i g_j} + \symup{\delta}_{ij}\,\bar{n}_i^{-1} & C_{\ell}^{\,g_i \mathrm{FRB}} \\[2mm]
        C_{\ell}^{\,g_j \mathrm{FRB}} & C_{\ell}^{\,\mathrm{FRB}} + \bar{n}_{\mathrm{FRB}}^{-1}
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}

with $\symup{\delta}_{ij}$ the Kronecker delta, which is written upright here to distinguish it from
the bias evolution exponent $\delta$. The shot noise appears only on the diagonal, since the
discreteness noise of two distinct tracer populations is uncorrelated, as already discussed in
\autoref{sec:cross_correlation_analysis}.

The derivatives entering \autoref{eq:fisher} are not available analytically and are evaluated by
central finite differencing,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite_difference}
    \frac{\partial C_{\ell}}{\partial \theta_i}
    \approx
    \frac{C_{\ell}(\theta_i + h_i) - C_{\ell}(\theta_i - h_i)}{2 h_i},
\end{equation}

with a step size of $h_i = 0.01\,\theta_i$, which is small enough for the spectra to respond
linearly and large enough to keep the difference well above the numerical noise of the projection
integral. The two parameters considered here are the amplitude $b_0$ and the evolution exponent
$\delta$ of the FRB host bias of \autoref{eq:frb_bias}; all cosmological parameters are held fixed
at the values listed in \autoref{tab:fiducial_cosmology}. The fiducial values and the corresponding
step sizes are summarized in \autoref{tab:fisher_setup}. Because the galaxy-galaxy block of
\autoref{eq:covariance_blocks} does not depend on the FRB bias, its derivative vanishes and only the
FRB row and column of $\partial C_{\ell}/\partial \theta_i$ are populated. The galaxy block
nevertheless enters the forecast through the inverse covariance, where it provides the reference
against which the cross-spectra are measured.

The sum in \autoref{eq:fisher} is carried out over the multipole range $\ell \in [10,\,1000]$
introduced in \autoref{sec:fiducial_cosmology}. The FRB-only forecast adopts the full FRB footprint
of $f_{\mathrm{sky}} = 0.9$ given in \autoref{tab:frb_surveys}, whereas the joint forecast is
restricted to the overlap with the KiDS field, $f_{\mathrm{sky}} = 0.0327$, corresponding to the
$\qty{1347}{\deg\squared}$ quoted in \autoref{sec:galaxy_redshift_distribution}. The joint analysis
thus covers roughly $27$ times less sky than the FRB-only one, so that any improvement reported
below is obtained in spite of, and not because of, the available survey area.

Inverting the Fisher matrix yields the parameter covariance $\mathrm{Cov} = F^{-1}$, whose diagonal
entries define the marginalized errors $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\mathrm{Cov}_{ii}}$. The confidence
contours in the $b_0$--$\delta$ plane are ellipses whose axes are aligned with the eigenvectors of
$\mathrm{Cov}$ and whose semi-axes are given by the square roots of the corresponding eigenvalues,
scaled by $\sqrt{\chi^2_2(p)}$ for a joint confidence level $p$. This amounts to a factor of $1.52$
for $p = \qty{68.3}{\percent}$ and $2.49$ for $p = \qty{95.4}{\percent}$~\cite{Coe_2009}. In order
to compress the two-dimensional constraint into a single number, the figure of merit

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fom}
    \mathrm{FoM} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\det \mathrm{Cov}}}
\end{equation}

is used, which is inversely proportional to the area of the confidence ellipse, so that larger
values correspond to tighter constraints~\cite{Albrecht_2006}.

The most immediate result is that the FRB auto-correlation alone does not constrain the host bias at
all. The marginalized errors range from $\sigma_{b_0} = 23.5$ for the most favorable configuration
to $\sigma_{b_0} = 101$ for the least favorable one, which exceeds the fiducial values of
\autoref{tab:frb_bias} by one to two orders of magnitude. The same holds for the evolution exponent.
Adding the galaxy sample reduces the errors to $\sigma_{b_0} = 1.27$--$2.42$ and
$\sigma_{\delta} = 1.77$--$3.76$, corresponding to an improvement by a factor of $17$ to $51$ in the
individual parameters and by a factor of $61$ to $449$ in the figure of merit. The joint analysis is
therefore not a marginal refinement of the FRB measurement but the reason a measurement becomes
feasible in the first place.

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:results_discussion}
The failure of the FRB-only forecast is a direct consequence of the shot-noise budget discussed in
\autoref{sec:shot_noise}. As \autoref{fig:frb_cell_shotnoise} shows, the Poissonian noise of a
sample of a few thousand bursts exceeds the clustering signal by about three orders of magnitude
across the entire multipole range, and even the deep configuration with $\num{5e4}$ bursts remains
noise-dominated by roughly two orders of magnitude. The observed covariance is then essentially
equal to the noise, $\hat{C}_{\ell} \approx N_{\ell}$, while the derivative in \autoref{eq:fisher}
is proportional to the much smaller signal. Each individual mode contributes only a minute amount of
information, and summing over the available modes does not compensate for this.

In the joint analysis the FRB field is no longer correlated with itself but with a second, far
denser field. Two properties of the cross-spectrum are responsible for the improvement. First, the
cross-spectrum carries no shot-noise contribution, so that the noise enters the corresponding term
of \autoref{eq:fisher} only through the two auto-spectra. The relevant noise level is therefore set
by the geometric mean of the FRB and the galaxy noise rather than by the FRB noise alone. Second,
the galaxy auto-spectra dominate over their own shot noise across the whole multipole range, since
the KiDS sample contains of order $\num{2e7}$ galaxies per steradian in each tomographic bin. One
factor of the large FRB noise is thus effectively replaced by a much smaller one, which is precisely
the multi-tracer argument that motivates the cross-correlation approach.

The orientation of the contours in \autoref{fig:fisher_comparison} is equally informative. In all
four panels the two parameters are anti-correlated, which follows directly from the functional form
of \autoref{eq:frb_bias}: the spectra are sensitive to the bias at the effective redshift of the
radial kernel, so that an increase of the amplitude $b_0$ can be compensated by a decrease of the
evolution exponent $\delta$ while $b_0\,(1+z_{\mathrm{eff}})^{\delta}$ remains unchanged. The
FRB-only contours are extremely elongated along this direction and constrain essentially a single
parameter combination, because the auto-spectrum projects the entire FRB kernel onto one effective
redshift. The joint contours are not only smaller but also visibly rounder. This is a genuinely
tomographic effect: each of the six galaxy bins samples the FRB kernel over a different redshift
range, as is evident from the bin dependence of the cross-spectra in \autoref{fig:cross_survey}, so
that the bias is probed at six different effective redshifts. The degeneracy between amplitude and
evolution is broken by this lever arm in redshift and not by the increased signal-to-noise ratio
alone.

Comparing the two FRB survey configurations, the deep sample performs better in every case, which is
expected given that it contains ten times as many bursts and therefore a shot noise lower by the
same factor. It is nevertheless worth noting how differently this factor propagates into the two
forecasts. In the FRB-only case the deep configuration improves $\sigma_{b_0}$ by a factor of three
to four, whereas in the joint case the gain amounts to only about $1.4$ to $1.5$. Once the
constraint is no longer limited by the FRB shot noise alone, increasing the number of bursts becomes
considerably less effective, and the radial overlap between the FRB and the galaxy sample takes over
as the limiting factor.

A comparison of the two progenitor classes requires some care, since their fiducial values differ.
The magnetar model yields the smaller absolute errors, but relative to its own fiducial amplitude the
neutron-star model is constrained equally well, with $\sigma_{b_0}/b_0 = 1.27$ against $1.13$ for the
deep configuration. The evolution exponent behaves differently: the delayed neutron-star channel is
assigned a shallow evolution of $\delta = 0.2$, so that a comparable absolute uncertainty corresponds
to a far worse relative precision. The physically relevant question, however, is not how well either
model is constrained in isolation but whether the two can be told apart. The two fiducial points are
separated by $\Delta b_0 = 0.5$ and $\Delta \delta = 0.6$, both of which lie comfortably inside the
$1\sigma$ contours of the joint forecast. With the KiDS footprint the cross-correlation therefore
improves the measurement by more than two orders of magnitude in the figure of merit, but it does
not yet reach the precision required to discriminate between a prompt magnetar and a delayed
neutron-star origin of the bursts.

This is not a statement about the method but about the available sky area. As pointed out above, the
joint forecast is confined to the $\qty{1347}{\deg\squared}$ of the KiDS field, and the Fisher
information of \autoref{eq:fisher} scales linearly with $f_{\mathrm{sky}}$. The natural way to
improve on the result presented here is therefore not a larger burst sample but a wider galaxy
survey, which is taken up in \autoref{sec:outlook}.
