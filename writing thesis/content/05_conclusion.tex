\chapter{Conclusion \& Outlook} \label{ch:conclusion}
The aim of this thesis was to assess whether the clustering of fast radio bursts can be used to
constrain the bias of their host population, and to what extent a cross-correlation with a
photometric galaxy survey improves such a measurement. To this end a complete forecasting pipeline
was developed. The nonlinear matter power spectrum was evaluated with \texttt{hmf} and represented
by a bicubic spline in comoving distance and wavenumber. Radial weight functions were constructed
for a mock FRB sample following \cite{Reischke_2021} and for the six tomographic bins of the final
KiDS-Legacy data release, and the corresponding angular auto- and cross-power spectra were obtained
from the Limber-approximated projection integral of \autoref{eq:limber}. The discreteness of both
tracer populations was accounted for through a Poissonian shot-noise term, and the resulting spectra
were propagated into a Fisher forecast for the amplitude $b_0$ and the evolution exponent $\delta$
of the FRB host bias.

The central result is that the FRB auto-correlation is of no practical use on its own. For the burst
numbers considered here the shot noise exceeds the clustering signal by two to three orders of
magnitude at every multipole, and the resulting marginalized errors are one to two orders of
magnitude larger than the parameters they are meant to constrain. Correlating the same burst sample
with the KiDS galaxies changes this picture entirely: the marginalized errors shrink by factors of
$17$ to $51$, and the figure of merit improves by factors of $61$ to $449$. Remarkably, this is
achieved on a footprint roughly $27$ times smaller than the one assumed for the FRB-only case. The
gain arises because the cross-spectrum itself is free of shot noise and because the dense galaxy
sample provides a low-noise reference field, so that the prohibitive FRB noise enters the forecast
only once instead of twice. In addition, the tomographic structure of the galaxy sample probes the
FRB kernel at several distinct effective redshifts and thereby breaks the degeneracy between the
amplitude and the redshift evolution of the bias, which is visible as a rounder confidence contour
in \autoref{fig:fisher_comparison}. At the same time the forecast shows that the KiDS footprint is
not yet sufficient to distinguish a prompt magnetar channel from a delayed neutron-star channel,
since the separation between the two fiducial models remains well inside the $1\sigma$ contours.

\section{Limitations} \label{sec:limitations}
The analysis presented here rests on a number of simplifying assumptions, each of which may affect
the forecast to some degree.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Both tracers are described by a linear, deterministic and scale-independent bias. In
    particular the galaxy bias of \autoref{eq:galaxy_bias} is evaluated once per tomographic bin and
    then held constant across its width, and the FRB bias of \autoref{eq:frb_bias} is assumed to
    follow a pure power law in $1+z$. On the smaller scales included in the multipole range this
    description is known to break down.
    \item The projection relies on the Limber approximation, which restricts the analysis to
    $\ell \gtrsim \mathcal{O}(10)$ and therefore excludes precisely those large angular scales on
    which the radial kernels differ most strongly between the two progenitor models.
    \item The forecast assumes a Gaussian likelihood and a purely Gaussian covariance. Non-Gaussian
    contributions arising from the nonlinear evolution of the density field, as well as super-sample
    covariance, are neglected. The Fisher errors therefore represent a lower bound on the true
    uncertainties.
    \item The FRB redshift distribution is a parametric model rather than a measured quantity. The
    burst numbers and the steepness parameter $\alpha$ of \autoref{tab:frb_surveys} are assumptions
    about future surveys, and the fiducial bias values of \autoref{tab:frb_bias} are motivated by
    the progenitor scenarios of \autoref{sec:progenitor_mechanisms} rather than measured.
    \item A fraction of the FRB host galaxies is presumably contained in the KiDS sample itself,
    which would introduce a small residual Poisson term in the cross-spectrum. Given the vast
    difference in number density between the two samples this contribution was neglected.
\end{itemize}

Finally, the incomplete sky coverage of a realistic survey is accounted for only through the sky
fraction $f_{\mathrm{sky}}$. A measurement on a masked sky does not yield the true angular power
spectrum but a mode-coupled pseudo-spectrum, whose deconvolution requires the full mode-coupling
matrix of the survey mask~\cite{Hivon_2002}. As this thesis is concerned with the theoretical
prediction of the angular power spectra rather than with their estimation from observed maps, no
mask is applied and no mode-coupling correction is performed; the effect of partial sky coverage
enters solely through the reduced number of independent modes per multipole.

\section{Outlook} \label{sec:outlook}
Since the Fisher information of \autoref{eq:fisher} scales linearly with $f_{\mathrm{sky}}$, the
most direct way to improve on the results of \autoref{ch:results} is to replace the KiDS field by a
substantially wider galaxy survey. The Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) at the Vera C. Rubin
Observatory will image about $\qty{18000}{\deg\squared}$ of the southern sky over ten
years~\cite{Ivezic_2019}, which corresponds to $f_{\mathrm{sky}} = 0.4363$ and thus to roughly
thirteen times the area available with KiDS. In order to illustrate what such a survey would add,
the pipeline of \autoref{ch:methodology} was run a second time with the ten-bin lens sample of the
LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration ten-year baseline, assuming a total number density of
$\qty{26.94}{\per\arcmin\squared}$ distributed equally among the bins~\cite{Mandelbaum_2018}. All
remaining ingredients, including the FRB configurations and the bias prescriptions, were left
unchanged, so that the two forecasts differ only in the galaxy sample. The resulting contours are
compared in \autoref{fig:fisher_kids_vs_lsst} and the corresponding numbers are listed in
\autoref{tab:lsst_outlook}.

The improvement is substantial. For the deep FRB configuration combined with the magnetar model the
marginalized error on the bias amplitude drops from $\sigma_{b_0} = 1.27$ to $0.25$, and the figure
of merit increases by a factor of $26$. Most of this gain is simply the larger footprint, as the
factor of thirteen in $f_{\mathrm{sky}}$ already accounts for the bulk of the difference; the
remainder is contributed by the finer tomographic resolution and by the deeper redshift coverage of
the LSST lens sample, which extends the useful overlap with the FRB distribution to higher
redshift. What matters physically is that the separation between the two progenitor models,
$\Delta b_0 = 0.5$ and $\Delta \delta = 0.6$, now lies outside the $1\sigma$ contours of the deep
configuration. A cross-correlation of a future FRB catalog with an LSST-like lens sample would
therefore be able to discriminate between a prompt and a delayed origin of the bursts, which the
KiDS configuration cannot. This conclusion should be read with the caveat that the equal split of
the total number density among the ten bins is an approximation, and that at matched sky area the
advantage would reduce to the tomographic and redshift-coverage terms alone.

Beyond a wider galaxy survey there are several directions in which the analysis presented here could
be extended.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The parametric redshift distribution of \autoref{eq:frb_nz} could be replaced by the
    measured distribution of an observed burst catalog. With the second CHIME/FRB
    catalog~\cite{CHIMEFRB2026} a sample of the size assumed for the shallow configuration is
    already available, so that the forecast could be turned into an actual measurement.
    \item The parameter vector could be extended from the two bias parameters to cosmological
    parameters such as $H_0$, $\symup{\Omega}_{m,0}$ and $\sigma_8$. The bias parameters would then
    act as nuisance parameters, and the question becomes how strongly the residual degeneracy
    between bias and cosmology degrades the cosmological constraints.
    \item Rather than treating the bursts as a pure number-count field, their dispersion measures
    could be used as weights. The dispersion measure integrates the free-electron density along the
    line of sight and therefore carries considerably more information per burst than its position
    alone~\cite{Reischke_2021, Shirasaki_2017}. This would also make the analysis sensitive to the
    distribution of baryons rather than only to that of the host galaxies.
    \item The Gaussian Fisher formalism could be replaced by a sampling of the full likelihood,
    which would reveal possible non-Gaussian features of the posterior and allow a consistent
    treatment of non-Gaussian contributions to the covariance.
    \item Finally, the linear bias model could be replaced by a scale-dependent halo-model
    description, in which the FRB hosts are populated into dark matter haloes according to their
    progenitor scenario. This would extend the analysis to the small scales that are currently
    excluded and would connect the measured bias more directly to the host halo masses.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, the results of this thesis show that the clustering of fast radio bursts becomes a
usable cosmological observable as soon as it is not analyzed in isolation. The bursts themselves are
far too sparse to support an auto-correlation measurement, but their cross-correlation with a dense
photometric galaxy sample recovers most of the information that is lost to shot noise. With the
survey footprints available today this suffices to constrain the host bias to order unity, and with
the footprints expected from the next generation of imaging surveys it should suffice to identify
the progenitor population itself.
