\chapter{Conclusion \& Outlook} \label{ch:conclusion}
This thesis assessed whether the clustering of fast radio bursts can constrain the bias of their
host population, and to what extent a cross-correlation with a photometric galaxy survey improves
such a measurement. A complete forecasting pipeline was developed: the nonlinear matter power
spectrum was combined with radial weight functions for a mock FRB sample and the six tomographic
bins of the KiDS-Legacy data release to produce angular auto- and cross-power spectra via the
Limber approximation. Poissonian shot noise was included for both tracer populations, and the
resulting spectra were propagated into a Fisher forecast for the amplitude $b_0$ and evolution
exponent $\delta$ of the FRB host bias.

The central result is that the FRB auto-correlation is of no practical use on its own. For the burst
numbers considered here the shot noise exceeds the clustering signal by two to three orders of
magnitude at every multipole, and the resulting marginalized errors are one to two orders of
magnitude larger than the parameters they are meant to constrain. Correlating the same burst sample
with the KiDS galaxies changes this picture entirely: the marginalized errors shrink by factors of
$17$ to $51$, and the figure of merit improves by factors of $61$ to $449$, achieved on a footprint
roughly $27$ times smaller than the one assumed for the FRB-only case. At the same time, the
KiDS footprint is not yet sufficient to distinguish a prompt magnetar channel from a delayed
neutron-star channel, as the separation between the two fiducial models remains well inside the
$1\sigma$ contours.

\section{Limitations} \label{sec:limitations}
The analysis rests on several simplifying assumptions that may affect the forecast.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Both tracers are described by a linear, deterministic, scale-independent bias. On the
    smaller scales included in the multipole range this description is known to break down.
    \item The Limber approximation restricts the analysis to $\ell \gtrsim \mathcal{O}(10)$,
    excluding precisely the large angular scales on which the radial kernels differ most strongly
    between the two progenitor models.
    \item The Gaussian likelihood and purely Gaussian covariance neglect non-Gaussian contributions
    from nonlinear structure growth. The Fisher errors should therefore be interpreted as optimistic
    lower bounds rather than as realistic uncertainties.
    \item The FRB redshift distribution and fiducial bias values are model assumptions rather than
    measured quantities.
    \item The small fraction of FRB host galaxies contained in the KiDS sample would introduce a
    residual Poisson term in the cross-spectrum; given the large difference in number density this
    contribution was neglected.
\end{itemize}

The analysis further simplifies the survey geometry by accounting for an incomplete sky coverage
only through the $f_{\mathrm{sky}}$ prefactor, while a real analysis would require a full
pseudo-spectrum treatment~\cite{Hivon_2002}.

\section{Outlook} \label{sec:outlook}
Since the Fisher information scales linearly with $f_{\mathrm{sky}}$, the most direct improvement
is to replace the KiDS field with a wider galaxy survey. The Legacy Survey of Space and Time
(LSST) at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory will image about $\qty{18000}{\deg\squared}$ of the
southern sky~\cite{Ivezic_2019}, corresponding to $f_{\mathrm{sky}} = 0.4363$, roughly thirteen
times the KiDS area. Running the pipeline with the ten-bin LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration
Y10 lens sample~\cite{Mandelbaum_2018}
while keeping all other ingredients unchanged yields the contours shown in
\autoref{fig:fisher_kids_vs_lsst} and the numbers listed in \autoref{tab:lsst_outlook}.

The improvement is substantial: for the deep magnetar configuration the error on $b_0$ drops from
$1.27$ to $0.25$ and the figure of merit increases by a factor of $26$. Most of this gain follows
directly from the larger footprint; the remainder comes from the finer tomographic resolution and
the deeper redshift coverage of the LSST lens sample. Physically, the separation between the two
progenitor models, $\Delta b_0 = 0.5$ and $\Delta\delta = 0.6$, now lies outside the $1\sigma$
contours of the deep configuration, meaning that a cross-correlation with an LSST-like sample
would be sufficient to discriminate between a prompt and a delayed origin of the bursts.

Beyond a wider footprint, several extensions of the analysis are conceivable:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The parametric FRB redshift distribution of \autoref{eq:frb_nz} could be replaced by
    the measured distribution of an observed catalog; with the second CHIME/FRB
    catalog~\cite{CHIMEFRB2026} a sample of shallow-configuration size is already available.
    \item The Gaussian Fisher formalism could be replaced by a full likelihood sampling, revealing
    non-Gaussian posterior features and allowing a consistent treatment of non-Gaussian covariance
    contributions.
    \item The linear bias model could be replaced by a scale-dependent halo-model description,
    connecting the measured bias to host halo masses and extending the analysis to smaller scales.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, the results of this thesis show that the clustering of fast radio bursts becomes a
usable cosmological observable as soon as it is not analyzed in isolation. The bursts themselves
are far too sparse to support an auto-correlation measurement, but their cross-correlation with a
dense photometric galaxy sample recovers most of the information lost to shot noise. With current
survey footprints this suffices to constrain the host bias to order unity; with next-generation
imaging surveys it should suffice to identify the progenitor population itself.
