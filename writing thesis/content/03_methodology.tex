\chapter{Methodology} \label{ch:methodology}

This chapter describes the analysis pipeline developed in this thesis to model the angular auto- and
cross-power spectra of Fast Radio Bursts and photometrically selected galaxies. The pipeline is
implemented in Python and follows the theoretical formalism established in
\autoref{ch:theoretical_background}. 


All numerical operations are carried out with \texttt{NumPy}~\cite{numpy} and
\texttt{SciPy}~\cite{scipy}. Background cosmological quantities such as the comoving distance and
the Hubble rate are evaluated with the \texttt{astropy.cosmology} module~\cite{astropy}, while the
linear and nonlinear matter power spectra as well as the linear growth factor are obtained from the
\texttt{hmf} package~\cite{hmf}. All figures are produced with \texttt{Matplotlib}~\cite{matplotlib}.

\section{Redshift Distributions} \label{sec:redshift_distribution}

\subsection{FRB Redshift Distribution} \label{sec:frb_redshift_distribution}
Since the FRB population considered in this thesis is not associated with individually measured
redshifts, its radial selection function has to be modelled. Following
Reischke, Hagstotz and Lilow~\cite{Reischke_2021}, the normalised FRB redshift distribution is
assumed to take the form

\begin{equation}\label{eq:frb_nz}
    n(z) \propto z^2 \exp(-\alpha z),
\end{equation}

where the parameter $\alpha$ controls the steepness of the high-redshift tail and thereby the
effective depth of the survey. The $z^2$ prefactor reflects the growth of the comoving volume
element at low redshift, whereas the exponential cutoff encodes the decreasing detectability of
bursts at large distances.

Two survey configurations are considered, both taken from~\cite{Reischke_2021} and summarised in
\autoref{tab:frb_surveys}. A shallow survey is characterised by $\alpha = 3.5$ and a total of
$N_{\mathrm{tot}} = \num{5e3}$ detected bursts, while a deep survey is described by $\alpha = 2.0$
and $N_{\mathrm{tot}} = \num{5e4}$ bursts. Both configurations are assumed to cover a sky fraction
of $f_{\mathrm{sky}} = 0.9$, consistent with the near-all-sky coverage achievable by wide-field
radio interferometers such as CHIME (\autoref{sec:fast_radio_bursts}).

The corresponding comoving distance distribution has to be transformed accordingly. Conservation of
the number of sources, $n(\chi)\,\mathrm{d}\chi = n(z)\,\mathrm{d}z$, yields

\begin{equation}
    n(\chi) = n(z)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}\chi},
\end{equation}

with the Jacobian $\mathrm{d}z/\mathrm{d}\chi = H(z)/c$ following from the definition of the
comoving distance in \autoref{sec:cosmological_background}.

\subsection{FRB Host-Population Bias} \label{sec:frb_bias}
As motivated in \autoref{sec:progenitor_mechanisms}, the two dominant progenitor classes are expected
to trace the underlying matter distribution differently. 
This difference is encoded in a redshift-dependent linear bias of the power-law form

\begin{equation}\label{eq:frb_bias}
    b(z) = b_0 \, (1+z)^{\delta},
\end{equation}

where $b_0$ denotes the bias amplitude at $z=0$ and the exponent $\delta$ parameterises its redshift
evolution. The fiducial values adopted for the two progenitor classes are listed
in \autoref{tab:frb_bias}. The magnetar channel is assigned a low
amplitude but a steep evolution, consistent with a young population whose host haloes become
progressively rarer, and therefore more strongly biased, towards higher redshift. The delayed
neutron-star channel is assigned a higher amplitude but a much shallower evolution, reflecting a
population that resides in massive hosts already at low redshift and whose clustering is largely
decoupled from the instantaneous star-formation rate.

\subsection{Galaxy Bias} \label{sec:galaxy_bias}
For the galaxy sample a constant-clustering bias model is adopted, in which the comoving clustering
amplitude of the sample is assumed to remain constant over the redshift range spanned by each
tomographic bin. Under this assumption the bias evolves inversely with the linear growth factor,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:galaxy_bias}
    b_g^{\,i} = \frac{0.95}{D_+(\langle z \rangle_i)},
\end{equation}

which is the standard prescription used in photometric survey forecasts~\cite{Johnston_2025}.

Here $D_+(z)$ denotes the linear growth factor normalised to $D_+(0)=1$, evaluated with
\texttt{hmf}~\cite{hmf}, and $\langle z \rangle_i$ is the mean redshift of the $i$-th tomographic
bin. The latter is computed from the tabulated distributions as the discrete first moment

\begin{equation*}
    \langle z \rangle_i
    =
    \frac{\sum_j z_j \, n_i(z_j)}{\sum_j n_i(z_j)}.
\end{equation*}

Since \autoref{eq:galaxy_bias} is evaluated at the mean redshift of the bin rather than at every
sampling point, each tomographic bin is assigned a single constant bias value. This is a deliberate
simplification: the bias is a slowly varying function across the width of an individual bin, so that
replacing it by its value at the effective redshift introduces an error that is small compared to
the uncertainty of the bias model itself. The resulting mean redshifts and bias values are listed in
\autoref{tab:galaxy_bins}.

\section{Radial Weight Functions} \label{sec:weight_functions}
The radial kernels $W^A(\chi)$ and $W^B(\chi)$ introduced in \autoref{eq:projected_field} weight the
three-dimensional density contrast along the line of sight. For a discrete tracer population whose
fluctuations are related to the matter density contrast by a linear bias, the kernel is given by the
product of the normalised radial selection function and the bias of the population.

For the FRB sample the weight function is therefore constructed on the redshift grid as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:weight_frb}
    W_{\mathrm{FRB}}(z) = b(z) \, n(z),
\end{equation}

with $n(z)$ from \autoref{eq:frb_nz} and $b(z)$ from \autoref{eq:frb_bias}. 

For the galaxy sample the corresponding kernel of the $i$-th tomographic bin reads

\begin{equation}\label{eq:weight_galaxy}
    W_g^{\,i}(z) = b_g^{\,i} \, n_i(z).
\end{equation}

Both kernels are subsequently transformed to comoving distance using the Jacobian introduced in \autoref{sec:frb_redshift_distribution}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:weight_chi}
    W(\chi) = W(z) , \frac{H(z)}{c},
\end{equation}

which gives the radial kernels entering the projection integral.

\section{Angular Power Spectrum Computation} \label{sec:angular_power_spectra}
The angular power spectra are computed from the Limber-approximated projection integral of
\autoref{eq:limber}.

For each multipole the integrand

\begin{equation*}
    \frac{W^{A}(\chi)\,W^{B}(\chi)}{\chi^2}\,
    P\!\left(k = \frac{\ell+1/2}{\chi},\, z(\chi)\right)
\end{equation*}

is evaluated on the comoving-distance grid corresponding to the redshift grid of
\autoref{sec:fiducial_cosmology} and integrated with the composite trapezoidal rule. 

\subsection{Auto-Correlation Analysis} \label{sec:auto_correlation_analysis}
For the auto-correlation of a single tracer population, the two kernels in \autoref{eq:limber}
coincide, $W^A = W^B$, and the projection integral reduces to a weighted line-of-sight average of
the matter power spectrum. Auto-spectra are computed for both FRB survey configurations and both
progenitor classes, as well as for each of the six tomographic galaxy bins.

In contrast to the cross-spectra discussed below, auto-spectra receive an additive Poissonian
shot-noise contribution from the discreteness of the tracer field, which is treated in
\autoref{sec:shot_noise}. \autoref{fig:frb_cell_shotnoise} illustrates the relative importance of
signal and noise for a representative FRB configuration, while \autoref{fig:frb_cell_comparison}
compares the signal-only spectra of the two survey configurations for both progenitor classes. The
galaxy auto-spectra of all six tomographic bins are shown in \autoref{fig:galaxy_cell_comparison}.

\section{Shot Noise and Survey Coverage} \label{sec:shot_noise}
Because both tracers are discrete point processes, the measured auto-spectra contain an additive
Poissonian noise term. For a sample of mean projected number density $\bar{n}$ per steradian, this
contribution is scale-independent and given by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:shot_noise}
    N_{\ell} = \frac{1}{\bar{n}}.
\end{equation}

For the FRB samples the number density follows from the total number of detected bursts and the
observed sky fraction listed in \autoref{tab:frb_surveys},

\begin{equation*}
    \bar{n}_{\mathrm{FRB}} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{tot}}}{4\pi f_{\mathrm{sky}}}.
\end{equation*}

Finally, the incomplete sky coverage of a realistic survey is accounted for only through the sky
fraction $f_{\mathrm{sky}}$. A measurement on a masked sky does not yield the true angular power
spectrum but a mode-coupled pseudo-spectrum, whose deconvolution requires the full mode-coupling
matrix of the survey mask~\cite{Hivon_2002}. As this thesis is concerned with the theoretical
prediction of the angular power spectra rather than with their estimation from observed maps, no
mask is applied and no mode-coupling correction is performed; the effect of partial sky coverage
enters solely through the reduced number of independent modes per multipole.
